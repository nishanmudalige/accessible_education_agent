Chapitre 1

The first chapter focuses on the greetings and farewells of French Canadians. We will explore “la bise” and the use of “tu” and “vous”.  We will examine the song “Bonjour mon ami” by Angèle Arsenault. This chapter will also highlight the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on the traditional greeting of “la bise”.

Culture canadienne

French-Canadian ways to say “Hello” and “Goodbye”

In French-speaking Canada, “bonjour” can be a greeting and a farewell; it can be used to say both “hello” and “goodbye.” Furthermore, the expressions “bon matin” and “bon après-midi” are greetings used in Canada to say, “good morning” and “good afternoon”, respectively. “Bon matin” and “bon après-midi” are not commonly heard in French-speaking European countries. For instance, in France, they do not say “bon matin.” To say, “good morning” in France, you simply say “bonjour.” “Bon après-midi” is used in France however, only when someone is leaving, not as a greeting.

 How to “faire la bise” in French Canada!

In French Canada, just like in other French-speaking countries, when people say hello, goodbye and congratulations to their friends and family, they will lightly kiss each other on the cheek (se faire la bise); however, worldwide, different countries “font la bise” differently.

In Canada, you kiss each cheek once for a total of two kisses, but in Europe, this number can vary. For example, they kiss three times in parts of Belgium, the Netherlands and Switzerland. Furthermore, there may be one to four kisses in different regions of France. Just like the number of kisses can vary, where these kisses start can, too; in Canada and Paris, we always start “la bise” on the left and finish on the right. “Tu” vs. “Vous” in Canada

The pronouns “tu” and “vous” both mean “you.” Generally speaking, “tu” is the singular form used when speaking to family members, friends or peers, and “vous” is used in both the singular form when talking to older people, authority figures and strangers and the plural form used when speaking to a group. This dichotomy is common in many French-speaking regions; however, French-speaking Canadiens use “tu” much more often. For example, Canadians may use “tu” to speak to a cashier they do not know.

When in doubt, to be respectful, start with “vous.” You can always switch to “tu” if the person you are speaking with tells you it is okay to do so.
